# Netcode: how shipped server-authoritative games do it, and what Rust and Bevy offer today

2026-11-06. The question, from v0's Server track ([docs/v0-tracks.md](../docs/v0-tracks.md#server-6)): there is no research on netcode and no design for prediction, what is sent or when, and D34 chose `renet` without one. This note surveys shipped practice and the current libraries, checked against the pinned Bevy 0.20. It informs [docs/netcode.md](../docs/netcode.md); it decides nothing.

Grades: **[P]** primary (the maker's docs, talk or source), **[S]** secondary (a write-up by someone else), **[C]** community claim with no primary source. Library versions and dates are crates.io's on 2026-11-06.

## In plain words

- Every shipped action game of our shape runs **one authority at a fixed tick**, sends **state, not inputs**, to the others, **predicts the local player** and **shows everyone else slightly in the past**. Nobody ships lockstep for a drop-in world.
- The two halves are separate techniques: prediction with replay for the body you steer, interpolation between snapshots for everything else.
- At scale the cost is **what you choose not to send**: by area, by priority, within a byte budget a packet.
- The one cautionary tale is **Valheim**: objects simulated by whichever client is near, relayed by the server. It is cheap to build and is why its fights lag with the worst connection present.
- In Rust, `renet` 2.0.1 is a plain crate (no Bevy), current, with netcode and Steam transports. The replication frameworks (`lightyear`, `bevy_replicon`) are Bevy crates that replicate ECS components; our authoritative state is mostly not in components (D28).

The method articles everyone cites: Fiedler's [Fix Your Timestep](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/), [Snapshot Interpolation](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_interpolation/), [Snapshot Compression](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_compression/) (quantised positions, smallest-three quaternions), [State Synchronization](https://gafferongames.com/post/state_synchronization/) (the priority accumulator), [Reliability over UDP](https://gafferongames.com/post/reliability_ordering_and_congestion_avoidance_over_udp/) (sequence, ack and a 32-bit ack field in every packet), [Reading and Writing Packets](https://gafferongames.com/post/reading_and_writing_packets/) (the bit packer, a reader that never trusts its input; his [yojimbo](https://github.com/mas-bandwidth/yojimbo) holds the code, BSD-3), and the [netcode standard](https://github.com/mas-bandwidth/netcode/blob/main/STANDARD.md) for connecting over UDP. Gambetta's [prediction and reconciliation](https://www.gabrielgambetta.com/client-side-prediction-server-reconciliation.html) is the plainest account of replay.

### What they agree on

1. **Fixed tick, numbered.** Inputs and state carry tick numbers; a client's input for tick T is applied on the server at T.
2. **Inputs are small, redundant and unreliable**: each packet repeats the last few, so a lost packet costs nothing.
3. **State is unreliable and the latest wins.** Either a whole snapshot delta-coded against an acked baseline (Quake, Source), or per-object versions resent until acked and chosen by priority (Tribes, Halo, Unreal). The second bounds bytes whatever the scene holds and costs nothing for things at rest, which is what a large world of mostly still things needs.
4. **Reliable ordered delivery only for what must not be missed or reordered**: commands, chat, world changes.
5. **Others are shown one or two updates in the past**; the delay is the price of smoothness and is a tuned number.
6. **The local body is predicted with the same movement code and replayed on a correction**, the picture smoothed over the error.
7. **Interest by area**, with hysteresis so a thing at the edge does not flicker.

### Where they differ, and which fits us

- **Snapshot delta or per-object state.** Whole snapshots suit small arenas of always-moving things. We have a large world, mostly at rest, aimed at many players (D52): per-object, most-recent-state, by priority.
- **Tick and send rates.** Shooters send every tick (60 to 128 Hz). Survival games get by on 20 Hz with interpolation. Our 64 Hz tick (D138) with a 32 Hz send is in between; the interpolation delay it implies (two updates, 62 ms) is nearer a shooter's than Minecraft's.
- **Hits.** With no player against player (D2), only player-on-creature blows need the server to rewind what the attacker saw; Source's bounded rewind is the method.
- **Who simulates creatures.** Valheim's client ownership is the outlier; every server-authoritative game simulates them on the server.

## Findings

1. The method is settled in the industry; the choices left are rates, delays and budgets, which are measured, not argued.
2. Per-object, most-recent-state replication by priority within a byte budget is the shape that scales toward D52 and costs nothing at rest.
3. Our rules already live outside Bevy (D28) and the mover is a plain, repeatable function of state and intent on a fixed tick (D138, D30): prediction by replay needs nothing new from it but a way to set a body to the server's state.
4. `renet` supports a plain crate today and is current; a transport seam with two lanes is all the design needs from it, and makes a deterministic in-memory transport with latency and loss possible for tests.
5. The Bevy replication frameworks would put the authority's state in components and need Bevy in every peer, against D28 and against cheap headless clients for load tests (multiplayer.md's thousand simulated players).
6. A reader of network input must be total: any bytes give a value or an error, never a panic or an unbounded allocation. That favours a small bit packer of our own, as Fiedler's, over a general serialiser.
7. Valheim's model is the one to avoid; Enshrouded and V Rising publish too little to copy.
