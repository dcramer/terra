# Performance systems: what would have caught 2 November's failures at once

2026-11-03. The developer, after a day of frame-time and load work at the lake: "those perf improvements are good enough for now. instead of pushing them further, I would want you to see if you can identify actual patterns, or systems that we should make sure are in place so that we can easier manage that performance in other places." Questions: which classes of failure did we hit, and how long did each take to find; what of our tooling caught them and what it missed; how shipped engines manage the same; which few systems would most cheaply prevent each class. Research and proposals only: nothing here is built.

Evidence: the day's handoffs (`~/.cache/terra-handoff/` play-lake-fps j1, j2, j6 to j13, perf-growth j1 and j2, load-time j1 to j8, perf-regress j1 and j2, seeds-j3), the coordinator's 11-02 lines and the commits they name. Times are the commits' (Pacific). Builds on [75 fps: where the frame goes](2026-10-15-75fps-budget.md), which measured the frame; this note is about the machinery round it.

## In plain words

- **Most of the day went to finding, not fixing.** Where a failure had a code fix, it was small (a memo, a pool's size, a cache); finding the cause took from one hour to twenty, across up to six jobs, and two were misread first.
- **Every expensive failure crossed a boundary no gate looked across**: a change meant for the load landed its cost in play (D603); a pool meant for the background took the foreground's cores; a probe meant to time a shader timed an undrawn ground or a setting sun.
- **Our budgets are one number and a few scattered constants.** D25's 13.3 ms is the only budget with an owner; ticks, load, GPU flatness are constants in three files (`FRAME_BUDGET_MS`, `bench/ticks.rs` `GPU_FLAT`, `bench/play.rs` `LOAD_MOST_S`). Nothing says how much of a frame creatures, streaming or the woods may take, so a regression is "the frame is slow" and a bisect, never "streaming is over its 2 ms".
- **Nobody owns the cores.** Bevy's pools, the streaming pool, building's pool, `par::map`, terrain's first view and five `std::thread::scope` sites (flora, sky occlusion, the recorder, building's draw and sections) each size themselves from `available_parallelism()` as if alone: some fifty threads on the bench's sixteen cores if all ran at once.
- **Shipped engines solve each of these with a small, boring system**: a per-frame time budget for streaming (Unreal's `s.AsyncLoadingTimeLimit`, Unity's `backgroundLoadingPriority`), priority classes in one job system with background work capped (Unreal's `ETaskPriority`, Godot's `low_priority_thread_ratio`), per-change perf runs with history and automatic bisects (Chromium's Pinpoint, Mozilla's Perfherder), and an always-on ring buffer that can be saved after the fact (Unreal's tail trace).
- **Proposed, ranked by cost against what each prevents**: one owner of threads (D606); a settled-probe harness on main (D609); budgets as data with a settle phase after load (D607); background work under a per-frame budget (D608); a perf row per landing with an automatic bisect, and fewer, shared benches on the worker (D610). Then cost counters and a tail capture, which need no decision. All five decisions are drafted as Leaning.

## 3. How shipped engines manage it

**Budgets a subsystem, as data.** Teams give each subsystem a share of the frame and check captures against it. Unreal's CSV profiler writes every stat a frame (`csvprofile start/stop`, `-csvCaptureFrames=N`, [docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/csv-profiler?application_version=4.27)) and its PerfReportTool turns a capture into a report; the AutomatedPerfTesting plugin (UE 5.5, [API](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Plugins/AutomatedPerfTesting/UAutomatedPerfTestControllerBase/TryStartCSVProfi-?application_version=5.5)) runs those captures from Gauntlet ([overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/gauntlet-automation-framework-overview-in-unreal-engine)) headless. The engine itself carries its targets as data (`FEnginePerformanceTargets`: the target frame, a hitch threshold and the ratio over the last frame a hitch needs, [API](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Engine/FEnginePerformanceTargets)). What a team's budget file looks like is not public; the shape (named stat, threshold, scene) is.

**Streaming and async loading under a budget a frame.** Unreal caps what loading may do on the game thread each frame: `s.AsyncLoadingTimeLimit` (ms a frame), `s.PriorityAsyncLoadingExtraTime` for high-priority loads, `s.LevelStreamingActorsUpdateTimeLimit` for registering a streamed level's actors, and one shared budget for both when `UseUnifiedTimeBudgetForStreaming` is set (`UEngine::GetUnifiedTimeBudgetForStreaming(DeltaSeconds, bHighPriority)`, larger behind a loading screen; [settings](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/streaming-settings-of-the-unreal-engine-project-settings), [API](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Engine/UEngine/GetUnifiedTimeBudgetForStreaming)). Unity does the same with one setting: `Application.backgroundLoadingPriority` caps the main thread's integration of async work at 2, 4, 10 or 50 ms a frame from Low to High ([docs](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/current/script-reference/unityengine/application/backgroundloadingpriority)). Godot loads on threads (`ResourceLoader.load_threaded_request`) and its worker pool's low-priority share (below). Guerrilla's "Streaming the World of Horizon Zero Dawn" (Decima) is listed in their publications but its content was not read here; Frostbite's job graph (Andersson, "Parallel Futures of a Game Engine", DICE 2010) is the same idea at the scale of the frame. The common rule: background work is cheap per frame by construction, budgeted where it touches the frame (the main thread's integration and the GPU upload), and the loading screen is only a larger budget.

**One job system, priorities, background capped.** Naughty Dog runs everything as jobs on fibers, one worker a core, with counters for dependencies and queues by priority (three in the open ports of the talk, FiberTaskingLib and NomadTasks; the slides were not read here) (Gyrling, "Parallelizing the Naughty Dog Engine Using Fibers", GDC 2015, [GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1022186/Parallelizing-the-Naughty-Dog-Engine)); Bungie's Destiny turned almost the whole frame into a job graph (Genova, "Multithreading the Entire Destiny Engine", GDC 2015, [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1022164/Multithreading-the-Entire-Destiny)). Unreal's task system has foreground (`High`, `Normal`) and background (`BackgroundHigh`, `BackgroundNormal`, `BackgroundLow`) priorities on one set of workers ([`ETaskPriority`](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Core/LowLevelTasks__ETaskPriority), [tasks systems](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/tasks-systems-in-unreal-engine)). Godot caps background work rather than ranking it: `threading/worker_pool/low_priority_thread_ratio` (0.3 by default) is the share of the pool's threads low-priority tasks may hold, "at least one worker thread … dedicated to low-priority tasks" and never more than the cores less one ([ProjectSettings.xml](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/blob/master/doc/classes/ProjectSettings.xml)); MIT, so its scheme (`core/object/worker_thread_pool.cpp`) could be ported with its notice. Bevy (MIT/Apache) has three pools sized by `TaskPoolOptions` (pinned 0.20 `bevy_app/src/task_pool_plugin.rs`: IO and async compute each 25% of the cores, 1 to 4 threads; compute the rest) and no priorities; we set none of it, so on 16 cores Bevy alone takes 4 + 4 + 8, before our own. perf-regress j1's fix (a quarter of the cores, `keep_to_this_thread`) is Godot's rule arrived at by bisect; building's pool (`terra_bases/src/layout.rs` `builders`) was made for the same reason earlier ("Bevy's pools have none, so building has a pool of its own").

**Per-change perf runs with history and bisect.** Chromium's perf bots upload every benchmark's measures to the Chrome Performance Dashboard, which charts them by commit and raises alerts; Pinpoint bisects a regression from a point on the chart to the change, across rolled dependencies ([bisects](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/da492f144feba8874ce6d25a9da170228763b21e/docs/speed/bisects.md); BSD, ideas only: our bisect is the nightly's). Mozilla's Perfherder alerts on a sustained change past a threshold a harness (2% for Talos and Browsertime) and sheriffs triage each alert to a bug ([perf sheriffing](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/testing/perfdocs/perf-sheriffing.html)). Both separate the measuring (once per change, shared by everyone) from the asking (an engineer reads the chart, not a bench of their own).

**Memory and draw budgets.** Unreal's Low-Level Memory Tracker tags every allocation by scope (`LLM_SCOPE`, custom tags in game code) and writes the tags' totals to CSV ([LLM](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/using-the-low-level-memory-tracker-in-unreal-engine), [Memory Insights](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/memory-insights-in-unreal-engine)), which a team checks against a budget a tag. Our bench already prints meshes, triangles, materials, mesh slabs and images at the first and last frame; they are gated on nothing.

**Capture on demand, after the fact.** Unreal keeps a tail trace, the last few seconds of events in a ring buffer (4 MB by default, `-tracetailmb`), and `Trace.SnapshotFile` writes it out without stopping ([trace](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/trace-in-unreal-engine-5), [quick start](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/trace-quick-start-guide-in-unreal-engine)). Tracy (BSD-3, D577) has an on-demand mode that records only once a viewer connects. The point is that a hitch in a player's session is caught by the build they were running, not by a profile build afterwards; our recorder (D576) already keeps fixed buffers a thread in every build and could save them.

## 4. Proposals, ranked

Ranked by what each costs against the failures it would have caught. A decision is drafted for each choice other work would build on, by the agent, Leaning.

### 1. One owner of threads (D606) — small, one job

**What**: one table, `terra_gen::par` (Bevy-free, the lowest layer that must understand it, D198), says each kind of work's share of the cores: the main and render threads keep theirs, the foreground compute pool the rest, background work (streaming, far cards, building's layouts) at most a quarter as Godot's ratio does, IO a small fixed count. `terra_world` sets Bevy's `TaskPoolOptions` from it and builds the streaming and building pools from it; `par::map` and every `std::thread::scope` site take their thread count from it, a background thread working alone (`keep_to_this_thread`, as now). A test sums the pools against the cores; the one-definition lint fails `available_parallelism` outside the table.
**Cost**: one job; the sites are five `thread::scope`s, terrain's first view, `par::map`, Bevy's options and two pools.
**Catches**: #3 at once (the pool could not be sized past its share), the fan-out of the far cards, and building's edit waiting 200 ms for a thread.

### 2. Settled probes, landed on main (D609) — small to medium, one or two jobs

**What**: the probe the lake jobs carried in patches (`perf-growth-j1/probe.rs`, the lush `probe.rs`, `swap.sh`, `lakeperf/*.sh`) as one tool on main (`cargo xtask probe`, its plugin in `terra_world::debug`). Before it reports a phase it holds the clock (`daylight.pace=0`), waits for the pipeline cache to have nothing pending and the opaque draw counts back to their most (j10's rule), fails a toggle (`hide:`, a tunable) that matched nothing, and reports the GPU's busy share and clocks beside its times. It runs variants A/B/A/B in one load, with an A/A pair for the noise floor. A lever being tried lands on main behind a tunable, off, its tests passing and the default unchanged, so the next job measures it on main instead of carrying a patch (the developer's word on experiments).
**Cost**: one or two jobs; most of the code exists in the handoffs.
**Catches**: #6a, b, d, e outright; #2 (clocks beside every time); #8 in part (one load for many variants, not a bench a side). #6c needs a different rule, in proposal 6.

### 3. Budgets as data, and a settle phase (D607) — medium, two jobs

**What**: one data file (`assets/data/perf/budgets.ron`, read by `xtask` and the game's readout) of budgets by scene and phase, replacing `FRAME_BUDGET_MS`'s copies, `GPU_FLAT` and `LOAD_MOST_S`: the frame (D25), ticks (D138), the load (D580's, once the developers set it), and a share a schedule or system group (the fixed loop, creatures, terrain's receive, woods, render prepare), background thread-seconds, GPU a pass group, and the world's content (meshes, triangles, mesh memory, draws). The recorder (D576) already times schedules in every build; groups are named spans. The bench times a **settle** phase, the first 30 s after the loading screen lifts, held to its own budget (looser on the frame, none on drift), instead of skipping it, and names the group over budget when a scene misses.
**Cost**: two jobs (the file and its reader; the groups and the settle phase).
**Catches**: #4 (the settle phase), #1 (the creatures' share names `creatures::walk` in the miss), #7 (a miss names the group, so a bisect is not the only way in), #10 (background thread-seconds), memory and draws before they grow.

### 4. Background work under a frame budget (D608) — medium, two jobs

**What**: one scheduler for work after load (`terra_world::streaming`): tasks in priority classes (near before far, ground before cards before cosmetic), fed to the pool a few at a time nearest first (perf-regress j2's `stream_far`), their results entering the world under a per-frame budget of main-thread ms and upload MiB (load-time j5's `ENTERING_BYTES`), the budget larger while the loading screen shows (Unreal's unified budget). It logs when each class drained (`Far view in N s`) and each class's thread-seconds by level, so a far chunk's cost against a near one's shows (#11) and the budget's falling with distance is checked.
**Cost**: two jobs; both halves exist as unlanded patches.
**Catches**: #10, #11's visibility, the far cards waiting 259 s, and #4's cost if the budget holds.

### 5. A perf row a landing, an automatic bisect, fewer benches (D610) — medium, one or two jobs, mostly tooling

**What**: a continuous row `perf` that runs a short set (lake-play, town-dusk, forest-run, castle; one run each) at each landing that touches what they read (`scope.rs`), coalescing landings that come while it runs, keeping each scene's numbers by commit. A failure after a pass bisects the range itself (the nightly's bisect, D411) and names the commit. A job's A/B reads main's side from the row's record at that commit, so it benches only its own side; the A/B of variants goes through the probe (2). Within D319 (benches have the GPU alone) and D445's planner.
**Cost**: one or two jobs in `tools/continuous` and `xtask`.
**Catches**: #7 at the landing, not 3.5 h later, its bisect done unattended; #8, about half of today's benches.

### 6. Cost counters (no decision yet) — small

Counters on the expensive pure primitives (`rise`, an exact node, `ground_at`, `height_m`), behind a feature, counted per output kind and printed by `lake_load_by_stage` and the bench: "rises a ground point 13.0". With budgets in the file of proposal 3 they would have caught #5 on the first probe. The probe that answers "is a test's distribution the real one" (#6c) is the same counter recording where the asks fall (by lattice phase, by level) and replaying them. Lives in `terra_gen` (the counter) and the crates that ask.

### 7. A tail capture (amends D576 later) — small

On a freeze, or when "the game is behind" fires, the recorder writes its last ten seconds of spans to `logs/` beside the log line, in every build, as Unreal's tail trace does. The developer's 4-fps log would have named the systems itself. Lives in `terra_world::recorder`.

### Outside the repo, for the developers

- The worker's `kernel.perf_event_paranoid` is 4, so no sampling profiler runs there (#9). Setting it to 1 (or `CAP_PERFMON` on `perf` and samply) is a system setting on the developer's box; named, not changed.

## Limits

- Times to find are from commit times and handoffs, not a log of when each agent started; a job's share of a queue wait is not separated from its work.
- Two causes are unconfirmed: town-dusk's +2 ms beside streaming (#10) and the far chunk's 5x (#11), both from perf-regress j2's arithmetic, not a probe.
- The engine sources are docs and talks; no engine's code was read. Unreal's (source-available) and Unity's are docs only; Godot's worker pool (MIT) is the one portable scheme named.
